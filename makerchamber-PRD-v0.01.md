### Problem Statement
Humans build understanding by doing and feeling, but abstract STEM concepts have no physical form to act on. So we teach them through symbols and formulas instead, and learners end up able to pass the test without any feel for what's actually going on.

### Who faces this problem? 
Anyone trying to build a real gut understanding of a technical concept from scratch. The clearest first group is non-technical and self-taught people learning AI/ML, like career switchers, PMs, and curious beginners who can read the words but never get the intuition. The same pain shows up across STEM education more broadly.

### How do we solve it? 
A game engine that builds playable games where the gameplay itself behaves like the concept you're learning. You don't read about tokenization, you act it out by breaking text into tokens, and the game tells you instantly what you got right, what you got wrong, and why.
Few things to make this work:  
(1) intrinsic integration: the learning is baked into the core mechanic instead of bolted on as a separate quiz or reward. 
(2) guided practice with immediate feedback: our hypothesized design walks the learner through the process and corrects them in real time. 
(3) Supplement to textbooks, not a replacement.  Right now, education jumps straight to symbols and demands the learner to build an intuition for them. Learners with strong memorization capabilities can pass without building deeper intuition. Others fall off the STEM wagon completely. The games run before or alongside the jargon that is introduced to build that gut sense first. As the learner progresses and improves, the game connects more deeply to the real notation, so they cross from the intuitive version to the actual thing without a jarring handoff. 

### Target Market 
(1) p0: self-directed learners plus the bootcamps, courses, and edtech products/creators serving them. 
(2) p1: Schools, universities and education boards globally once learner outcomes and engagement is validated. 

### GTM Motions
(1) Content and product led. The games are inherently shareable and make strong standalone demos, so individual games act as free, viral entry points. 
(2) Edtech platforms, bootcamps, independent educational creators selling courses who want to embed interactive concept-games in their material.

### Revenue Model
(1) Freemium for individual learners with a paid tier
(2) B2B licensing to education providers. 
(3) Longer term, a creator marketplace where others build and sell concept-games on the engine.

### Key Risks to validate
(1) Who will really pay for it? Commercially the biggest open question which needs more validation.
(2) Learner engagement: Is the game even fun enough for a learner to stick with it? 
(3) Impact on learning outcomes: Does it actually teach? We need to measure real learning gains, not just enjoyment. Can the learner articulate what they learnt with confidence/clarity? 
(4) Does the sequencing claim hold? Our bet that playing the game first makes later instruction land harder is plausible and theory-backed, but it isn't directly proven yet. We need to test it head to head.
(5) Does it generalize? Tokenization maps cleanly to a game because it's a step-by-step procedure. We need to find how many concepts have an honest game-able form and where the mapping breaks down.
(6) Can it scale beyond hand-crafted? The hard part isn't the game, it's finding the action that honestly matches the concept. We need to learn how much of that can be templatized versus done by hand.
(7) Why can't generalized AI models do this?
