Before a model processes a single word, it consults its Ledger — a fixed vocabulary of fragments built during training. The model read billions of sentences and counted which pieces of text appeared together most often. The most frequent fragments earned their own entries. The Ledger doesn't contain full words — it contains the pieces that showed up enough to be worth registering. Common short words are in there whole. Longer or rarer words aren't, so they get broken down until their parts are. The cuts don't follow meaning. They follow what was frequent in the training data.

Every LLM run starts with exactly this — raw text split into tokens before any processing begins.
What you just did is called tokenization, and it's performed by a component called a tokenizer. The tokenizer takes raw text, breaks it into fragments found in the Ledger, and converts each fragment into a number — its ID. The transformer doesn't recognize text the way you do, associating words with meaning directly. It receives a sequence of IDs. As you progress through the levels, you'll see what the model does with those IDs — and why that turns out to be enough.

The Ledger (called a vocabulary) is frozen at training time: the same vocabulary is used for every run, every sentence, every prompt. Different models use different tokenizers — GPT-4 uses one called cl100k, Claude uses its own. The splits can differ, but the principle is the same: frequency-based fragments, built once, used everywhere.
