The Oracle

The last traveler in the sequence reaches the far gate. They've been through every floor. Now they must speak.

The Oracle's question is always the same: what comes next? The answer is never a word. It's a distribution — a probability over every token in the vocabulary. One gets drawn. That word is the output.

You'll predict the next token for a sentence you've followed through every level of this city. Then you'll see what temperature does to how the model commits.

How scoring works

Score = 100 − average gap between your probability and the model's, for each token. Putting all your confidence on the right word isn't the goal — accurate calibration is. Being 40% confident on "shore" and 30% on "bank" (when those are genuinely the most likely) scores higher than being 100% confident on the wrong word.

Temperature

The model has a ranked list of candidates. Slide left and the ranking collapses to near-certainty — one token takes almost everything. Slide right and the ranking flattens — every candidate gets a more equal share of the probability. That slider is called temperature. Drag it and watch.

What temperature doesn't change

No matter where you move the slider, the model's ranked list of candidates stays the same — the same words in the same order, with the same underlying scores. What changes is how committed the model is when it draws. At low temperature it almost always picks its top choice. At high temperature it gives its second, third, and fourth choices a real shot. This is why raising temperature feels like "creativity" — the model isn't learning anything new, it's just reaching further down its own list.

Why models hallucinate
Even at its most confident, the model never puts 100% of its weight on a single word — there is always a sliver of probability on wrong answers. Usually that sliver is too small to matter. At high temperature, it grows large enough to get drawn. And here is the strange part: once a wrong word is chosen, it becomes the input for the next prediction. The next distribution shifts. Then the next. One unlikely draw can cascade into sentences that sound plausible but are entirely fabricated. That cascade — not a flaw in the math, just a consequence of never being perfectly certain — is what hallucination is.

What you've learned
At every step, the model spreads probability across its entire vocabulary and draws one word. Then it does it again. And again. What you experience as a coherent sentence, a working function, or a direct answer is the result of that process running thousands of times — each draw shaped by everything every layer computed for every token in the sequence. There is no lookup, no retrieval, no stored answer. Just a distribution, sampled once, whose output becomes the next input. This is called autoregressive generation.
